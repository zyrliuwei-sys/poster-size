Choosing a poster size before you design saves more time than adjusting a finished layout later. The right canvas depends on where the poster will be viewed, how far away people will stand, and whether the final work is printed or shown on a screen.

## Start with the job

For a wall poster, begin with the physical space and the viewing distance. An 18x24 poster is a flexible medium format for art prints, classroom graphics, and smaller events. A 24x36 poster gives a headline and image more room and is a reliable choice for public displays, event posters, and storefront campaigns.

For international print work, the A series is a useful reference. A4 and A3 are familiar paper sizes, while A2, A1, and A0 move into larger poster territory. North American formats such as 11x17, 18x24, and 24x36 are often easier to source from local print shops in the United States and Canada.

## Match the poster ratio

The aspect ratio is the relationship between width and height. An 18x24 poster uses a 3:4 ratio, while 24x36 uses 2:3. Keeping the ratio consistent lets you create alternate sizes without cropping the main composition. If your design has a portrait photograph, a 2:3 or 3:4 canvas usually leaves more natural room around the subject than a square crop.

Digital posters need the same decision even though the output is measured in pixels. A social post may use a square 1:1 canvas, a story uses 9:16, and a presentation uses 16:9. Pick the display format first, then choose a pixel size large enough for the platform and the intended text scale.

## Leave room for print

Print files need bleed when color or imagery runs to the edge. Many printers request an additional 0.125 inches on each side, but the exact requirement varies. Keep headlines, logos, and faces inside a safe margin so they do not feel cramped or get too close to the trim line.

Resolution is a viewing-distance decision. For a close-viewed art print, 300 DPI is a useful starting point. A 24x36 poster at 300 DPI is about 7200 × 10800 pixels. A display poster viewed from farther away can often use a lower effective resolution, but do not make small text carry information it cannot support at that distance.

## Check your canvas before exporting

Before sending a file to print, confirm the finished dimensions, orientation, color mode, bleed, and export format. Compare the final file against a poster size chart rather than relying on the name of a template. That quick check catches the most expensive mistakes: a swapped width and height, a cropped focal point, and a file that is too small for the wall.
